### 3. Interview "Must-Master" 80/20 Cards

#### Card 1: Factor Return Decomposition Mechanics
- **The Core Question**: *"How do you prove that an active mutual fund charging 85 bps is actually just delivering commoditized beta?"*
- **Winning Answer**: Explain multi-factor regression with investable ETF benchmarks: $R_F - R_f = \alpha + \beta_1(R_{SPY} - R_f) + \beta_2(R_{IWD} - R_{IWF}) + \beta_3(R_{MTUM}) + \epsilon$. If the gross alpha $\alpha$ is $+1.0\%$ but the fund charges $0.85\%$ in expense ratio and has heavy loadings on large-cap momentum, the **Pure Residual Alpha** is only $0.15\%$ with high idiosyncratic risk. An investor can replicate 90% of the return profile using low-cost passive ETFs at 10 bps, saving 75 bps annually.

#### Card 2: Cosine Return Similarity vs. Correlation in Fund Substitution
- **The Core Question**: *"Why would you use Cosine Return Similarity instead of Pearson Correlation when screening for substitute funds?"*
- **Winning Answer**: Pearson correlation centers returns around their respective means, meaning two funds could have a correlation of 0.98 even if one fund has twice the volatility or a permanent negative drift. Cosine similarity treats return series as geometric vectors in high-dimensional time-space ($\frac{\mathbf{r}_A \cdot \mathbf{r}_B}{\|\mathbf{r}_A\| \|\mathbf{r}_B\|}$), capturing both directional co-movement and magnitude alignment without distorting mean shifts. We pair Cosine Similarity ($\ge 0.96$) with annualized Tracking Error ($\le 2.0\%$) to guarantee true portfolio fungibility.

#### Q2: High-Dimensional Cosine Similarity vs. Distance Metrics
- **Interviewer**: *"When screening 10,000 funds for pairwise substitution, why use Cosine Return Similarity rather than Euclidean distance?"*
- **Top 1% Answer**:
  > *"Euclidean distance is sensitive to scale and volatility magnitude: two funds with identical investment strategies where one uses 1.2x beta leverage will have a massive Euclidean distance despite identical correlation. Cosine similarity calculates the cosine of the angle between two normalized return vectors ($\frac{\mathbf{r}_A \cdot \mathbf{r}_B}{\|\mathbf{r}_A\| \|\mathbf{r}_B\|}$), isolating pure directional harmony independent of volatility scaling. We then combine Cosine Similarity ($\ge 0.96$) with annualized tracking error ($\le 2.0\%$) and beta differential to ensure that the substitute is both structurally co-moving and risk-matched."*

#### Q3: Handling the 45-Day Reporting Lag in Form 13F
- **Interviewer**: *"SEC Form 13F filings are published up to 45 days after quarter-end. How do you handle this lag when modeling institutional ownership?"*
- **Top 1% Answer**:
  > *"We treat 13F data as a lagging snapshot that requires decay weighting. First, I compute historical portfolio turnover for each reporting institution: an RIA with 15% annual turnover has a high position half-life, meaning their 45-day-old 13F is a highly reliable proxy for current holdings, whereas a quantitative hedge fund with 300% turnover cannot be relied upon. Second, for mutual funds, I cross-reference monthly Form N-PORT filings which provide 30-day updates. Third, I build automated confidence scores into our addressable market metrics, clearly distinguishing high-conviction, low-turnover holdings from stale positions."*

#### Q4: Solving Optimization Instability in Portfolio Construction
- **Interviewer**: *"Classic Markowitz Mean-Variance optimization often produces extreme, unstable portfolio weights. How do you mitigate this in production?"*
- **Top 1% Answer**:
  > *"Classic mean-variance optimization is an 'error maximizer' because it inverts an ill-conditioned covariance matrix and over-allocates to assets with estimation error. In production, I implement three techniques: First, Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage to regularize the sample covariance matrix toward a single-factor market benchmark. Second, L2 regularization (Ridge penalty) or maximum weight constraints ($\le 10\%$) in CVXPY. Third, Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP), which uses graph-theory tree clustering on the correlation matrix to allocate risk weights without requiring matrix inversion, producing significantly more robust out-of-sample Sharpe ratios."*
